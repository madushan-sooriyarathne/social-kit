## 1. Translating brand adjectives into visual rules

Write each adjective as a rule the crew can execute. One example: "warm" becomes golden-hour key light, wood and linen textures, lifted shadows, a slightly warm white balance. Cover these dimensions:

| Dimension | Decide |
|---|---|
| Light | Natural vs controlled; hard vs soft; time of day; contrast ratio |
| Palette and grade | Brand colours plus neutrals; saturation level by category (rich for hedonic, restrained for utilitarian); one master look |
| Lens language | Wide and immersive, natural 35 to 50 mm honesty, or compressed telephoto intimacy |
| Angle tendency | Eye level for trust, low for aspiration, overhead for order |
| Movement (video) | Locked-off calm, gimbal glide, handheld energy |
| Casting | Real staff, guests or customers vs models; age and diversity matching the actual audience |
| Styling and props | Materials, colours to avoid, props that signal use |

Block's contrast and affinity: give hero and campaign frames high contrast in one or two components (tone, colour, space or line), keep library frames in affinity so the set feels cohesive.

## 2. Camera angle and height

| Angle | Communicates | Use for | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eye level | Honest, peer-to-peer | People, founders, testimonials, SaaS | Consensus |
| Low (looking up) | Power, aspiration, monument | Hero product, architecture, luxury exteriors | Controlled: favoured mainly when viewers are not processing carefully |
| High (looking down) | Overview, context, or vulnerability | Room layouts, table spreads, site context | Controlled: meaning depends on context |
| Overhead / flat lay | Order, design, inventory | Food spreads, kits, collections | Consensus |
| Dutch | Unease, energy | Rarely; usually wrong for luxury and real estate | Consensus |
| POV | Immersion, "this could be you" | Experiences, app use, hands-on product | Supported by Hartmann self-reference finding |
| Over-the-shoulder | Shared perspective, screen context | SaaS, consultations | Consensus |
| Aerial / drone | Scale, awe, location | Resorts, villas, developments | Consensus |

Camera height conventions: interiors about chest height or lower (roughly 1.0 to 1.3 m), adjusted so counters and floor plane read naturally; table food at diner's eye line (about 45 degrees) or overhead; product heroes slightly below centre for presence.

## 3. Shot sizes and library coverage

Ladder: extreme wide or establishing, wide, full, medium wide, medium, medium close-up, close-up, extreme close-up or detail, plus inserts and cutaways for video.

Coverage matrix per location or product:
1. Establishing: where we are, sense of place
2. Hero: the single best expression, composed with copy space
3. People-in-place: human interaction with the space or product
4. Product or feature: information that answers a buyer question
5. Detail and texture: materials, craft, food close-ups
6. Hands-in-use: the brand-selfie perspective (higher purchase intent and CTR)
7. Portrait: real staff, founders, makers
8. B-roll and cutaways for video

## 4. Composition and subject placement

- **One clear subject.** The eye should land on it within a second. Remove clutter (feature complexity hurts brand attention); structured arrangement (design complexity) helps.
- **Rule of thirds** as a starting heuristic and copy-space tool only.
- **Centred and symmetrical** for architecture, corridors, pools, beds and luxury product; symmetry signals order and precision.
- **Depth through layers:** foreground, midground, background; leading lines; frames within the frame. Deep space for hero and establishing shots, flat space for graphic product shots.
- **Lead room and headroom:** space in the direction a subject faces or moves; tight headroom in close-ups, natural in wides.
- **Copy space and crops:** for each hero, capture one version with clean negative space on the side the web layout needs (16:9 hero) and one with space top or bottom (4:5 and 9:16). Keep critical subject matter away from the frame edges that social interface elements cover. Shoot wider than the tightest crop needs. Confirm safe-zone dimensions by test upload or an independent production guide.

## 5. Gaze and eyelines

- Product or brand must be seen: model looks at the product. Eye-tracking shows longer fixation on product and logo and better recall.
- Connection and trust (portraits, founder, testimonials): direct gaze, ideally with a genuine smile.
- Self-projection (hospitality, experiences): faces partial or turned toward the view so viewers imagine themselves there.
- Conversion assets: a hands-only version with no face.
- Capture the alternatives on set; test them later.

## 6. Lenses and depth of field (full-frame equivalents)

| Focal length | Effect | Use |
|---|---|---|
| 14 to 24 mm | Expands space, stretches edges | Tight interiors, bathrooms, drone. Avoid misrepresenting room size; over-promising leads to bad reviews |
| 24 mm tilt-shift | Straight verticals without distortion | Architecture and interiors |
| 35 to 50 mm | Natural perspective, honest | Lifestyle, people-in-place, honest room views |
| 85 to 135 mm | Flattering portraits, separation | Portraits, details |
| 70 to 200 mm+ | Compression, candid distance | Architectural details, stacking a property against landscape, candid people (relaxes non-models) |

- Spaces: keep rooms sharp front to back (f/8 to f/11 or focus stacking).
- Food and product details: shallow focus for texture, but keep the key feature sharp.
- Verticals: keep the camera level, shift rather than tilt, then correct residual lean in post. Mike Kelley names converging verticals as the most common flaw in architectural work and corrects even slight misalignment. **Consensus.**

## 7. Lighting

- **Natural first, controlled always:** window and ambient light for authenticity, flash or LED to fill shadows and balance interior and exterior exposure. Blend ambient and flash exposures for interiors.
- **Golden hour:** warm exteriors, pools, people. **Blue hour or twilight:** exteriors with interior lights on, the conventional hero for luxury real estate and hospitality. Schedule both from `scripts/light_windows.py`.
- **Key, fill, back:** key sets mood and direction; fill controls contrast; backlight or rim separates subjects (steam, glassware, hair).
- **Hard vs soft:** hard for drama and texture (fashion, sport); soft for calm, luxury, approachability (hospitality, wellness, SaaS people).
- **Colour temperature:** switch off or gel conflicting sources; mixed tungsten, daylight and LED creates casts that read as amateur.
- **By subject:** interiors with window light plus bounced fill, lamps on; food with back or side light and small fill; glossy products with large diffused sources, flags and gradients; people with a soft key at about 45 degrees and catchlights.

## 8. Colour and grade

- One palette, one grade, one white-balance philosophy per campaign, applied to stills and video alike.
- Category rule: richer colour for hedonic and experiential categories (travel, food); restraint for utilitarian and premium-technical (SaaS, B2B, some luxury).
- Warmth, exposure and contrast lifts associated with higher engagement in one large Flickr study; directional.
- Check skin tones and brand colours against swatches after grading. Don't justify palettes with colour-psychology folklore.

## 9. Directing talent

- Give actions, not poses: "pour your partner a glass and tell her about your morning".
- Use a longer lens from a distance so people forget the camera; run several takes of the same action; play music; shoot the warm-up.
- Prompt real conversation to get spontaneous changes of expression (a right-brain feature linked to effective ads).
- Hands: clean, natural, holding or using the product as a person would.
- Cast real staff and customers where possible; generic stock-looking people are ignored by viewers.
- Wardrobe: two or three palette-matched options per person, no logos, no fine stripes (moiré).

## 10. On-set priorities and variations

- Tiers: A must-have (heroes, contracted deliverables, light-dependent shots), B should-have (library), C nice-to-have (experiments). Under time pressure protect A, drop C, compress B by capturing several crops per setup.
- Roles: photographer or DP owns exposure, focus, light and lens execution; art director owns story, styling, composition intent, brand fit and coverage. Agree who calls "moving on".
- One client approver at the tethered monitor; review selects at the end of each block, not continuously.
- Variation checklist per setup: with and without people; horizontal and vertical; tight and wide; copy space left, right and top; gaze at camera and at product; with and without props; a B-roll clip of the same scene.

## 11. Perfect-shot checklist (judge before moving on)

1. Purpose: answers a buyer question or carries the brief's emotion
2. Subject clarity: eye lands on the subject within a second
3. Clutter removed: cords, reflections, stray objects, colour noise
4. Light intentional: highlights and shadows controlled, no colour contamination, catchlights
5. Lines: verticals vertical, horizon level
6. Lens honesty: space represented truthfully
7. Focus and depth of field right for the sector
8. Gaze and interaction directed where attention should go; hands natural
9. Expression authentic, best of the take sequence reviewed
10. Brand fit: palette, wardrobe, props, grade
11. Crop safety: survives 16:9, 4:5 and 9:16, copy space present
12. Variations captured
13. Technical: exposure, no unintended blur, enough resolution for the largest deliverable
14. Approval logged for A-tier shots

## 12. Common mistakes

Converging verticals; ultra-wide distortion; decorative scenes with no information; clutter; mixed colour temperatures; stiff posed talent; no copy space; no vertical versions; over-retouching that removes credibility; judging by likes alone.
