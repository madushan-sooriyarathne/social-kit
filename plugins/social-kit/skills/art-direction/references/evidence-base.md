# Evidence Base for Art Direction (independent sources only, current to November 2026)

Strength labels: **Controlled** = experiment, eye-tracking or peer-reviewed econometric study; **Dataset** = independent analysis of a large effectiveness or attention database; **Consensus** = craft convention shared across several named practitioners and educators; **Single** = one named practitioner's view.

Platform-authored creative guidance (Meta, TikTok, YouTube/Google, LinkedIn, Pinterest, Snapchat) is excluded. Studies co-produced with a platform or sold by a research vendor are flagged.

## 1. Quality and taste alignment

- **Zhang, Lee, Singh and Srinivasan (Management Science, 2022):** panel of 7,423 Airbnb properties over 16 months. Verified professional photos raised occupancy 8.98%. Once 12 interpretable image attributes from photography and marketing literature were controlled for, the professional-photo effect became insignificant: the photographers added value by improving quality and aligning images with buyer taste. Implication: the art director's job is to codify that taste. **Controlled.**
- **Li and Xie (Journal of Marketing Research, 2020):** adding an image to a tweet raised retweets 119% (air travel) and 213% (SUVs), likes 87% and 151%. Professional photos were most preferred; screenshots attracted least engagement. Raters described high-quality images as having a clear theme subject, following composition rules, with proper lighting and high resolution. **Controlled.**

## 2. Information-carrying vs decorative imagery

- **Nielsen Norman Group eye-tracking (Jakob Nielsen):** big feel-good images that are purely decorative were ignored. Photos of products and real people were scrutinised. On one team page users spent 10% more time on portraits than bios, although bios took 316% more space. Generic stock people were ignored. Product thumbnails were studied intensely when they helped users tell products apart and ignored when they did not. **Controlled.**
- Implication: shoot details that answer buyer questions (how big is the shower, what does the dashboard show), not generic textures.

## 3. People, faces, hands and gaze

- **Bakhshi, Shamma and Gilbert (CHI 2014, 1.1M Instagram photos):** photos with faces 38% more likely to get likes and 32% more likely to get comments. **Controlled.**
- **Li and Xie (2020):** faces raised Twitter engagement 38% to 291% but had no effect on Instagram. **Controlled; conflicts with the above.**
- **Hartmann, Heitmann, Schamp and Netzer (JMR, 2021, 250,000+ brand image posts, 185 brands):** consumer selfies (face plus brand) earned the most likes and comments. Brand selfies (an unseen consumer's hand holding the product) produced more purchase-intent comments and higher display-ad click-through. A lab study pointed to self-reference: viewers imagine themselves more easily without a stranger's face. **Controlled.**
- **Gaze, eye-tracking (Hutton and Nolte 2011; Sajjacholapunt and Ball 2014; Adil, Lacoste-Badie and Droulers 2018):** when a model looks at the product rather than the viewer, people look longer at the product, logo and rest of the ad, and recall improves. **Controlled (mostly print and banner ads).**
- **Wang, Wedel, Huang and Liu (Information and Management, 2018):** smiling plus direct gaze raises arousal much more, especially for Chinese consumers; smiling averted gaze produces deeper processing of product information. **Controlled.**
- Implication: faces for awareness and social; hands-in-use for conversion assets; gaze at product when the product must be seen, direct gaze for connection and trust. Shoot both.

## 4. Complexity, colour and filters

- **Pieters, Wedel and Batra (Journal of Marketing, 2010):** feature complexity (dense colour, luminance and edge detail, i.e. clutter) hurt brand attention and ad attitude; design complexity (an elaborate but structured arrangement) helped. **Controlled.**
- **Overgoor, Rand, van Dolen and Mazloom (2022, firm-generated Instagram images):** inverted-U for feature complexity, U-shape for design complexity; optimising both could improve engagement about 19% per the co-author. **Controlled.**
- **Li and Xie (2020):** colourful images helped air-travel posts; monotone images with less colour variation did better for SUVs (utilitarian). Colour richness suits hedonic and experiential categories; restraint suits utilitarian and premium-technical ones. **Controlled.**
- **Bakhshi, Shamma, Kennedy and Gilbert (ICWSM 2015, 7.6M Flickr photos):** filtered photos 21% more likely to be viewed and 45% more likely to be commented on; filters raising warmth, exposure and contrast helped most. Single platform, directional. **Controlled.**

## 5. Camera angle

- **Meyers-Levy and Peracchio (JMR, 1992):** products shot from a low angle were rated more favourably than from a high angle, mainly among viewers with low processing motivation. Weaker for considered purchases (real estate, B2B). **Controlled, dated.**
- **Peracchio and Meyers-Levy (JCR, 2005):** a high, downward angle can carry weakness or a positive meaning depending on context. **Controlled.**

## 6. Composition rules

- **Amirshahi, Hayn-Leichsenring, Denzler and Redies (Art and Perception, 2014):** aesthetic ratings correlated only weakly with viewers' judgments of rule-of-thirds compliance and not at all with computed compliance. Highly rated photos and paintings did not follow the rule more often. **Controlled.**
- Use thirds mainly to create copy space for layouts. Golden ratio has no stronger support.
- **Bruce Block, The Visual Story:** greater contrast in a visual component (space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement, rhythm) creates more visual intensity; affinity creates less. **Consensus.**

## 7. Video attention, hooks and branding

- **Karen Nelson-Field, Amplified Intelligence:** 85% of digital ads get under 2.5 seconds of active attention (130,000 ad views). In scrollable media attention drops sharply after about two seconds; by a late first brand impression, about half the paid reach has scrolled away. Long ads in scroll feeds are largely wasted. **Dataset (vendor).**
- **Teixeira, Wedel and Pieters (Marketing Science, 2010; eye tracking, about 2,000 participants, 31 commercials):** brand presence alone increases zapping; pulsing the brand briefly and repeatedly at the same total exposure cut zapping 8 to 10%. **Controlled.**
- **Teixeira, Wedel and Pieters (JMR, 2012, facial coding plus eye tracking on online video ads):** surprise and joy concentrate attention and retain viewers. Practical form: a quick surprise at the start, then a longer stretch of joy. **Controlled.**
- **System1 and IPA, Achtung! (2020):** ads that held attention over the first six seconds were more likely to show right-brained features up front. Co-produced with Facebook and based on small samples (10 vs 10 ads). **Dataset, directional.**
- **VCCP Media and Nelson-Field (2025):** brands with strong distinctive assets can register in about 1.5 seconds. Agency-sponsored; applies mainly to brands with established assets. **Single/Dataset, weak.**

## 8. Emotion and right-brained features

- **Binet and Field (IPA Databank, 996 campaigns):** emotional campaigns produced more brand and business effects than rational ones and over the long term were almost twice as likely to deliver top-box profit growth. Rational messaging won only in short-term direct response. **Dataset.**
- **Orlando Wood (System1; Lemon, IPA 2019; Look Out, IPA 2021):** right-brain features (characters with agency, dialogue, a clear sense of place, people touching, spontaneous changes of expression, melodic music, one scene unfolding) associate with higher System1 Star ratings and stronger long-term effects. Left-brain features (flatness, abstracted product, obtrusive words, voiceover, monologue, product-centricity) associate with weaker ones. Look Out flags close-up product shots and the stare (facial frontality) as features that push viewers away. **Dataset (vendor sells the testing; the brain metaphor is contested).**

## 9. Captions and sound

- **Szarkowska et al. (PLOS ONE, 2024, n=168):** subtitled video with sound off significantly reduced comprehension, enjoyment and immersion vs sound on. Non-ad clips. **Controlled.**
- **Jones and Jennings (CALICO Journal, 2022):** more time on captions meant less visual uptake. Captions compete with visuals. **Controlled.**
- Implication: tell the story visually first, add concise captions, and make the audio reward those who listen. Widely quoted "X% watch with sound off" figures come from vendors and platforms; don't rely on them.

## 10. Measurement

- Tie each asset set to a business metric: direct bookings or occupancy (hospitality), qualified enquiries (real estate), demo requests or trials (SaaS), add-to-cart and conversion (ecommerce), plus brand search and recall over time.
- Hartmann et al.: likes can diverge from purchase intent and CTR. Binet and Field: short-term response understates long-term brand effects.
- Run simple A/B tests on the brand's own channels where findings conflict: hands-only vs face, gaze at product vs at camera, hook variants, colour-rich vs restrained grade.

## 11. Myths and weak claims

- **"Always follow the rule of thirds."** Compliance does not predict aesthetic ratings. Use it for copy space.
- **"The golden ratio makes images more beautiful."** No robust evidence.
- **"Faces always increase engagement."** Platform-dependent, and can lower purchase intent vs hands-only shots.
- **"Orient the product to the viewer's right hand to raise purchase intent."** Elder and Krishna (JCR, 2012) reported it; an eight-experiment replication in the International Journal of Research in Marketing did not reproduce it. Treat as a minor tweak at most.
- **"Low angle always flatters products."** Conditional on low involvement; high angles can be positive.
- **Colour psychology rules ("blue builds trust", "red sells").** Effects depend on category; universal meanings are folklore.
- **"Save the big reveal for the end."** Late branding loses most scroll viewers and is wasted if they leave.
- **"Simpler is always better."** Clutter hurts, but structured design complexity helps.
- **"X% watch with sound off."** Vendor and platform statistics; design for both states.
- **"Likes equal success."** See measurement.

## 12. Expert disagreements and how to resolve them

| Topic | One side | Other side | Working rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct vs averted gaze | Eye-tracking: gaze at product lifts product and brand attention | Wang et al.: smiling direct gaze raises arousal; portrait convention favours eye contact; System1 links the stare to weaker TV ads | Match gaze to the shot's job; capture both |
| Faces vs no faces | Bakhshi: strong face effect on Instagram | Li and Xie: none on Instagram; Hartmann: faces lower purchase intent | Faces for awareness and social; hands-in-use for conversion |
| Early branding | Nelson-Field: brand early because attention decays | Teixeira: brand presence triggers zapping | Brand early but briefly, then pulse |
| Left/right brain | Wood's feature lists predict effectiveness in a large dataset | Neuroscience framing contested; vendor sells the test | Use the feature lists, not the brain metaphor |
| Polish vs authenticity | Airbnb and Li and Xie reward professional quality | NN/g and Hartmann reward real, relatable imagery | Professionally crafted, authentic-looking content, not stock gloss |
| Rule of thirds | Taught across practitioner education | Weak academic support | Starting heuristic and copy-space tool only |

## 13. Caveats

- Most engagement studies use Twitter/X and Instagram data from 2010 to 2020. Treat effect sizes as directional.
- System1, Amplified Intelligence and VCCP findings come from commercial firms; Achtung! was co-produced with Facebook.
- Gaze studies mostly use print and banner ads; angle studies are from 1992 and 2005.
- Craft guidance (light, lens, continuity, direction) is expert consensus with an indirect link to engagement.
- No peer-reviewed study directly tests a 1 to 3 second hook on TikTok, Reels or Shorts; hook guidance extrapolates from attention-decay data and first-seconds coding.
